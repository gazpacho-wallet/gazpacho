First run

Welcome to Gazpacho

A wallet for small, fast bitcoin payments (Lightning). You hold your own keys. You share one on-chain output, called a factory, with a host and other members. The host pays to set it up; you start with a balance of zero and a channel you can receive into.

Nothing works until you are in a live factory. You cannot pay or receive before that. Getting in takes three short steps, then a wait until a lobby starts.

What can go wrong, and what the app does

None of these has happened in factory 12. Each would show here and in Activity.

  • An old state was published. The app answers it with the newer state. It does this by itself; there is no choice to make.
  • The host was caught cheating. The app answers, takes what the rules give you, and adds the host to the hosts you avoid. It says what it did and what you received.
  • A neighbour left alone. Part of your branch is then on chain. The app tells you what changed for you, for example that leaving alone became cheaper or quicker.
  • The funding was un-confirmed by a reorganisation of the chain. The app stops signing until it confirms again.
  • Host not answering. Payments fail. You can still leave alone.

It is the arrangement that follows from the points on which the three research reports (wallets; node dashboards and markets; lobbies and consent) agree without having read each other, plus the best single borrowing for each remaining problem. Four owner decisions made after the research override it in places; each override is marked in the tables below.

    Five first-level entries, because that is what a labelled phone tab bar holds and what all three reports converge on once their overlaps are merged. Home and Activity are in every report. Lobbies is in every report. Conditions is first-level because two of three reports say plainly that putting it in Settings is the weak point of their own concepts. Pay is first-level because the gap check found that the map "had no home for the thing a wallet is for". Watching did not get a tab (the wallet report proposed one): its state is in the strip on every screen and its dates are on Home, so a tab would mostly repeat them. Nothing is more than two levels down; the items joined with "|" are tabs of one page.

    Hosting is a sixth first-level entry, set apart beside Settings: in the side menu on a wide screen, in the header on a phone (so the phone tab bar keeps five labelled tabs). It is always there, as owner decision 1 requires, but it sits apart because most people will use it rarely. Until the person hosts something it shows only what hosting needs and a "Write terms" button, and nothing about hosting appears on Home. There is no host mode and no role question anywhere.

    Strengths

    • The conditions screen answers its own question. Change one number and the fit count, the excluding limit, the lobby list and the strip all change together.
    • One sentence at a fixed place always says what the app is doing and why, and the stop button is beside it. The same sentence carries waiting, signing, refusal and "nothing fits".
    • Every area has one home, and the same fit check appears before, at, and after commitment, so the record can be compared with what was promised.
    • Hosting is one tap away for everyone, yet a person who never hosts sees only a calm starting page there and nothing about hosting on Home. Host forms never share a screen with my conditions.
    • The whole-range fit check makes adjustable terms safe by default: the "Fits" mark cannot become false between joining and the ready check.

    Weaknesses

    • The strip is tall on a phone: with the app header it takes about a quarter of a small screen. It is the price of putting four facts and a control on every screen.
    • Two things are called a profile: my starting set of conditions (Cautious, Everyday, Wide) and the host's label for a lobby (Safety, Standard, Starter, Circle). The research wanted the first, the map defines the second. They need different words.
    • Because it is a blend, it has no single familiar model. A Phoenix user and a Sparrow user will each find half of it unfamiliar.
    • Pay, as one first-level entry with four tabs, hides Receive one tap deeper than most wallets do; the Home buttons cover it only on Home.
    • The highest fee per payment lives under Pay rather than Conditions. That follows the map's note, but it bends the "one screen for what I accept" idea.
    • A range in every fit-check row makes the table wider and harder to read on a phone.
    • Hosting sits apart from the five main entries. On a phone it is a header link, not a tab, which may read as less important than it is for someone who hosts daily.
    • Settings / Wallet looks thin, because coins moved to Pay and display moved to Application.

    What I would change after seeing it

    • Collapse the strip on a phone to one line (the sentence and Pause), with the chips behind a tap, and test whether people still notice a lagging server.
    • Rename my profiles to something that cannot be confused with lobby profiles, for example "starting sets", or ask whether lobby profiles are needed at all once every limit is explicit.
    • Put Receive beside Pay in the phone tab bar as a split button, or make the Pay screen open on a two-button choice.
    • Put the next hosting date in the Home next-action sentence when it is sooner than the member date, so one sentence still covers both roles.
    • Test the wording of "Open the app by". A rolling date is honest but odd on a timeline; it may read better as a state, "Covered until…".
    • Try the fit check on a phone as a list of sentences for the rows that fail and a single "11 other rows fit" line, opening to the full table.
